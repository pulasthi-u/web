Introduction

Let us begin by imposing a 3D coordinate system on the world around us. Choose some arbitrary point \(o\) in this world space, and let it serve as the origin. Let \(\mathbf{i}\), \(\mathbf{j}\), \(\mathbf{k}\) be the usual unit basis vectors, with \(\mathbf{k}\) pointing out of the page, so as to form a right-handed coordinate system.

We shall consider the problem of capturing an image of a planar surface in the world (such as a billboard or banner) with a camera, which we will assume is placed at \(o\).

Without loss of generality, the image plane, i.e., the plane on which the camera forms its image, can be considered to be a plane parallel to the \(\mathbf{i}\mathbf{j}\) plane, at a distance \(d\) into the page.

Let us impose a 2D coordinate system on this image plane too.

We will choose the point pointed to by the vector \(-d\mathbf{k}\) as its origin, and call it \(o'\) (so \(\overrightarrow{oo'} = -d\mathbf{k}\)). Then, we make the observation that as the plane is parallel to the \(\mathbf{i}\mathbf{j}\) plane, it can be spanned by \(\mathbf{i}\) and \(\mathbf{j}\); any point on it can be located by moving some distance \(\lambda'\) along \(\mathbf{i}\), and some other distance \(\mu'\) along \(\mathbf{j}\), starting from the origin, with negative distances interpreted as moving in the opposite direction.

We will identify such a point with the coordinates or ordered-pair \((\lambda', \mu')\).

Finally, we shall impose a 2D coordinate system on the planar surface (billboard, banner, etc.) that we are trying to capture.

For this, choose some arbitrary point \(O\) on it to serve as the origin. Let the vector to \(O\) from \(o\), i.e., \(\overrightarrow{oO}\), be \(O_x\mathbf{i} + O_y\mathbf{j} + O_z\mathbf{k}\).

Let also \(\mathbf{X}=X_x\mathbf{i} + X_y\mathbf{j} + X_z\mathbf{k}\) and \(\mathbf{Y}=Y_x\mathbf{i} + Y_y\mathbf{j} + Y_z\mathbf{k}\) be two vectors lying on this surface, spanning it. Then, any point on this plane can be located by moving a distance \(\lambda\) along \(\mathbf{X}\), and \(\mu\) along \(\mathbf{Y}\), starting from \(O\), just as we did on the image plane.

Let \(P\) be such an arbitrary point on this plane, with coordinates \((\lambda, \mu)\).

Now, where on the image plane would point \(P\) in the world appear?

We can imagine a ray of light somehow emanating from \(P\), and making its way to the camera at \(o\). In doing so, the ray will intersect the image plane at some point, and that is exactly where it would appear in the image formed by the camera.

Let us call that point on the image plane \(p\). Then, the question becomes, what are the coordinates of \(p\), in the coordinate system of the image plane?

Suppose \(p\) has coordinates \((\lambda', \mu')\) on the image plane. Then, the position vector from \(o\) to \(p\) is \(\overrightarrow{op} = \overrightarrow{oo'} + \overrightarrow{o'p} = \lambda'\mathbf{i} + \mu'\mathbf{j} - d\mathbf{k}\).

The vector \(\overrightarrow{oP} = \overrightarrow{oO} + \overrightarrow{OP} = \overrightarrow{oO} + \lambda\mathbf{X} + \mu\mathbf{Y}\) would describe a ray of light such as the one mentioned above, so \(p\) is in fact, the intersection of \(\overrightarrow{oP}\) with the image plane.

Hence, \(\overrightarrow{op}\) and \(\overrightarrow{oP}\) must be parallel, implying the existence of some scalar \(\nu\) such that \(\nu \cdot \overrightarrow{op} = \overrightarrow{oP}\).

Putting everything above together, this yields the following equation: \[ \begin{align*} \nu \cdot \left( \lambda'\mathbf{i} + \mu'\mathbf{j} - d\mathbf{k} \right) &= \overrightarrow{oO} + \lambda\mathbf{X} + \mu\mathbf{Y} \\ \lambda'\nu\mathbf{i} + \mu'\nu\mathbf{j} - d\nu\mathbf{k} &= (O_x\mathbf{i} + O_y\mathbf{j} + O_z\mathbf{k}) + \lambda (X_x\mathbf{i} + X_y\mathbf{j} + X_z\mathbf{k}) + \mu (Y_x\mathbf{i} + Y_y\mathbf{j} + Y_z\mathbf{k}) \\ &= (O_x + \lambda X_x + \mu Y_x)\mathbf{i} + (O_y + \lambda X_y + \mu Y_y)\mathbf{j} + (O_z + \lambda X_z + \mu Y_z)\mathbf{k} \\ 0 &= \left( O_x + \lambda X_x + \mu Y_x -\lambda'\nu\right)\mathbf{i} + \left(O_y + \lambda X_y + \mu Y_y -\mu'\nu\right)\mathbf{j} \\ &\quad + \left(O_z + \lambda X_z + \mu Y_z + d\nu\right)\mathbf{k}, \end{align*} \] which require \[ \begin{gather} O_x + \lambda X_x + \mu Y_x -\lambda'\nu = 0, \label{eq1} \\ O_y + \lambda X_y + \mu Y_y -\mu'\nu = 0, \label{eq2} \\ O_z + \lambda X_z + \mu Y_z + d\nu = 0. \label{eq3} \end{gather} \]

We would like something in the form of a function which accepts the coordinates on the planar surface being photographed as input, i.e., \(\lambda\) and \(\mu\), and produces the coordinates on the image plane that they will appear at, i.e., \(\lambda'\) and \(\mu'\), as output.

It doesn't take long to notice that the three equations above lend themselves to a neat representation in matrix-vector form as follows: \[ \begin{pmatrix} X_x & Y_x & O_x \\ X_y & Y_y & O_y \\ X_z & Y_z & O_z \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \lambda \\ \mu \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \nu\lambda' \\ \nu\mu' \\ -\nu d \end{pmatrix}, \notag \] which is conveniently in the input-output form we seek, with inputs on the left and outputs on the right.

We can do a little better by moving the \(d\) in the right-hand side over to the left by dividing both sides of \(\eqref{eq3}\) by \(-d\), with the understanding that \(d \ne 0\); which is a reasonable assumption that translates to requiring that the image plane not pass right through the camera.

That would result in the following matrix-vector relationship: \[ \begin{pmatrix} X_x & Y_x & O_x \\ X_y & Y_y & O_y \\ -\dfrac{X_z}{d} & -\dfrac{Y_z}{d} & -\dfrac{O_z}{d} \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \lambda \\ \mu \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \nu \lambda' \\ \nu \mu' \\ \nu \end{pmatrix}. \label{main-homo-mat-vec-extend} \]

Given the coordinates \((x, y)\) in some 2D coordinate system, we call an ordered triple \((x', y', a)\), where \(x=\dfrac{x'}{a}\) and \(y=\dfrac{y'}{a}\), the homogeneous coordinates of \((x, y)\). So \((x, y, 1)\), \((2x, 2y, 2)\), or most generally, \((ax, ay, a)\), for any \(a \ne 0\) will be homogeneous coordinates of \((x, y)\). We make this definition for later mathematical convenience.

Accordingly, \(\begin{pmatrix} \lambda & \mu & 1 \end{pmatrix}^\top\) are homogeneous coordinates of \(P\) on the plane being photograped, and \(\begin{pmatrix} \lambda' & \mu' & 1 \end{pmatrix}^\top\) are homogeneous coordinates of \(p\) on the image plane.

Let us denote them by \(\mathbf{x}\) and \(\mathbf{y}\) respectively.

If we denote the matrix above by \(\mathbf{H}\), then \(\eqref{main-homo-mat-vec-extend}\) can be rewritten more compactly as \[ \mathbf{H}\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{y}. \] We call such a matrix \(\mathbf{H}\), a homography matrix.

Actually, while we are still at it, let us go off a bit of a tangent, and consider the following more general case.

If \(O\), \(\mathbf{X}\), and \(\mathbf{Y}\) are still as defined above, and we introduce a third vector \(\mathbf{Z} = Z_x\mathbf{i} + Z_y\mathbf{j} + Z_z\mathbf{k}\) so as to span all of 3D space, then any point \(P\) in the world can be described w.r.t \(O\) by some position vector \(\lambda\mathbf{X} + \mu\mathbf{Y} + \nu\mathbf{Z}\); so that \(P\) has coordinates \((\lambda, \mu, \nu)\) in the frame described by \(\mathbf{X}\), \(\mathbf{Y}\), and \(\mathbf{Z}\).

Then, if \(p\) is where \(P\) must appear on the image plane and \(\overrightarrow{op}\) is also as defined above, \[ \begin{align*} \alpha \cdot \left( \lambda'\mathbf{i} + \mu'\mathbf{j} - d\mathbf{k} \right) &= (O_x\mathbf{i} + O_y\mathbf{j} + O_z\mathbf{k}) + \lambda (X_x\mathbf{i} + X_y\mathbf{j} + X_z\mathbf{k}) + \mu (Y_x\mathbf{i} + Y_y\mathbf{j} + Y_z\mathbf{k}) \\ &\quad + \nu (Z_x\mathbf{i} + Z_y\mathbf{j} + Z_z\mathbf{k}), \end{align*} \] which in a very similar manner as above leads to the matrix-vector expression \[ \begin{pmatrix} X_x & Y_x & Z_x & O_x \\ X_y & Y_y & Z_y & O_y \\ -\dfrac{X_z}{d} & -\dfrac{Y_z}{d} & -\dfrac{Z_z}{d} & -\dfrac{O_z}{d} \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \lambda \\ \mu \\ \nu \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \alpha \lambda' \\ \alpha \mu' \\ \alpha \end{pmatrix}. \]

Finding a Homography Matrix

Based on the results above, if we start knowing exactly what \(\mathbf{X}\), \(\mathbf{Y}\), \(O\) and \(d\) are, then we can construct \(\mathbf{H}\) and use it to transform any point on the plane surface in question to the image plane.

Consider instead the following situation. We know the coordinates of a few points on a certain surface, in terms of some unknown coordinate frame imposed on it, and we have an image of that surface available from which we can find the coordinates (in a typical \(xy\) coordinate system we impose on the image) of where those same points landed.

This is the case where we have some set of \(\mathbf{x}\)'s and their corresponding \(\mathbf{y}\)'s, i.e., we have a set of correspondences, but we do not know the \(\mathbf{H}\) relating them; so the problem then is, what is that \(\mathbf{H}\)?

We will start out with an \(\mathbf{H}\) consisting of nine unknowns for each of its nine entries, like so: \[ \mathbf{H} = \begin{pmatrix} h_{11} & h_{12} & h_{13} \\ h_{21} & h_{22} & h_{23} \\ h_{31} & h_{32} & h_{33} \end{pmatrix}. \]

Now, if we have the correspondence \((\lambda_1, \mu_1) \mapsto (\lambda_1', \mu_1')\), then \[ \begin{gather} h_{11}\lambda_1 + h_{12}\mu_1 + h_{13} = \nu_1\lambda_1' \label{eq4} \\ h_{21}\lambda_1 + h_{22}\mu_1 + h_{23} = \nu_1\mu_1' \label{eq5} \\ h_{31}\lambda_1 + h_{32}\mu_1 + h_{33} = \nu_1. \label{eq6} \end{gather} \] Substituting for \(\nu_1\) in \(\eqref{eq4}\) and \(\eqref{eq5}\) using \(\eqref{eq6}\) give \[ \begin{align*} h_{11}\lambda_1 + h_{12}\mu_1 + h_{13} &= \left( h_{31}\lambda_1 + h_{32}\mu_1 + h_{33} \right)\lambda_1' \notag \\ h_{21}\lambda_1 + h_{22}\mu_1 + h_{23} &= \left( h_{31}\lambda_1 + h_{32}\mu_1 + h_{33} \right) \mu_1', \notag \end{align*} \] or equivalently, by rearranging, \[ \begin{align} h_{11}\lambda_1 + h_{12}\mu_1 + h_{13} - h_{31}\lambda_1\lambda_1' - h_{32}\mu_1\lambda_1' - h_{33} \lambda_1' &= 0 \label{eq7} \\ h_{21}\lambda_1 + h_{22}\mu_1 + h_{23} - h_{31}\lambda_1\mu_1' - h_{32}\mu_1\mu_1' - h_{33} \mu_1' &= 0 \label{eq8}. \end{align} \]

We have just obtained two equations relating the nine unknown entries of the matrix.

These equations place two constraints on the unknowns, leaving seven degrees of freedom—meaning that we can choose the values of some seven of them arbitrarily, and have the remaining two be determined by the equations (that em-dash was not AI-generated, I put it there myself 🥲😅).

Notice that it is very much possible for there to exist infinitely many choices of \(\mathbf{X}\), \(\mathbf{Y}\), and \(O\) such that the single point \(P\) in the corresponding coordinate frame still appears at the same point \(p\) on the image plane.

By choosing seven values arbitrarily, we are effectively picking out one of these solutions. The two equations only serve to ensure that even with such arbitrary choices, \(P\) does still map to \(p\).

We can do "better" than choosing values arbitrarily. By introducing more correspondences, we can keep reducing the number of degrees of freedom, and try to discover the exact underlying \(\mathbf{X}\), \(\mathbf{Y}\), and \(O\), such that not only do the chosen \(P\)'s land at their corresponding \(p\)'s, but any arbitrary point on the planar surface described by \(\mathbf{X}\) and \(\mathbf{Y}\) lands at the expected position on the image plane.

As we showed above, a single correspondence gives two equations, leaving seven degrees of freedom. Hence, it should be clear that with four correspondences, we will have eight equations, with just one degree of freedom.

Now, we have a problem. Eight equations are not enough to discover each unknown exactly—so we need more than four correspondences—but if we include any more, we'll have more equations than unknowns, i.e., the system becomes overdeterminate.

Let us collect these nine unknowns in the long vector \[ \mathbf{h} = \begin{pmatrix} h_{11} & h_{12} & h_{13} & h_{21} & h_{22} & h_{23} & h_{31} & h_{32} & h_{33}\end{pmatrix} ^ \top. \notag \] This lets us combine \(\eqref{eq7}\) and \(\eqref{eq8}\) into the following matrix-vector equation: \[ \begin{pmatrix} \lambda_1 & \mu_1 & 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 & -\lambda_1\lambda_1' & -\mu_1\lambda_1' & -\lambda_1' \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & \lambda_1 & \mu_1 & 1 & -\lambda_1\mu_1' & -\mu_1\mu_1' & -\mu_1' \end{pmatrix} \mathbf{h} = \mathbf{0}. \notag \]

With three more correspondences, the matrix on the left-hand side will grow in a similar fashion, giving a total of eight equations relating the nine unknowns. Let us call that final resulting \(8 \times 9\) matrix \(\mathbf{A}\), so our final expression is \[ \mathbf{A}\mathbf{h} = \mathbf{0}. \]

The system as it now stands, with four correspondences is underdeterminate, as we have one more unknown than equations. We could introduce one more correspondence, but that would make the system overdetermineate, with one more equation than unknowns.

We address this situation by stopping just before the system becomes overdeterminate (we stop at four correspondences), and imposing further constraints of a different nature on the unknowns until it becomes determinate.

In this case, we start with the observation that the "perfect" solution for \(\mathbf{A}\) must satisfy \(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{h}=\mathbf{0}\), i.e., \(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{h}\) must perfectly equal \(\mathbf{0}\). So if some other \(\mathbf{A}'\) is such that \(\mathbf{A}'\mathbf{h}\) is not exactly equal to \(\mathbf{0}\), but is really close to it, then it's probably not a bad estimate of the perfect \(\mathbf{A}\).

We shall measure closeness of two vectors \(\mathbf{a}\) and \(\mathbf{b}\) in terms of the \(2\)-norm of their difference, i.e., the quantity \(\lVert \mathbf{a} - \mathbf{b} \rVert\). Then, if we seek \(\mathbf{A}\) such that \(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{h}\) is as close as possible to \(\mathbf{0}\), we seek to minimize the distance \(\lVert \mathbf{A}\mathbf{h} - \mathbf{0} \rVert = \lVert \mathbf{A}\mathbf{h} \rVert\). If the solution to this problem is \(\mathbf{A}^\star\), then \[ \mathbf{A}^\star = \arg\min_\mathbf{A} \lVert \mathbf{A}\mathbf{h} \rVert. \]

But \(\lVert \mathbf{A}\mathbf{h} \rVert = \left(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{h}\right)^\top\left(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{h}\right)\)

Introduction

Signals and systems are very common in nature. Many physical quantities that we encounter everyday in nature can be viewed as signals, and many natural phenomena can be viewed as processes or systems that respond to such signals, by generating new signals. This ubiquity of signals and systems has motivated the development of general tools to represent and analyze them methodically and conveniently.

Conclusion

We conclude by noting that all the transforms discussed above are indeed methods of scanning a signal \(x(t)\), and decomposing it into simpler signals, by extracting the frequency components present in it. By performing such a decomposition, we make the analysis and manipulation of the signal simpler, and extract information out of the signal that might otherwise not be immediately visible.
